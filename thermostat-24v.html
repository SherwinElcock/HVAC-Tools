Meter checks at the thermostat and air handler

Set your meter to AC volts. Work in this order so you split the problem in half each step.

  1. R to C at the control board should read about 24–28V. Zero means no transformer output, a blown board fuse (usually 3 or 5A), an open door switch, or an open float or condensate switch in the R line.
  2. Same R to C check at the thermostat. Good at the board but dead at the thermostat means a broken or disconnected wire in between.
  3. With a call for cooling, measure Y to C at the board. 24V means the thermostat is doing its job, so move to the equipment.
  4. Measure across the contactor coil at the condenser. 24V there with the contactor not pulling in means a bad coil or contactor. No voltage there means a broken Y or C wire outside, or an open safety switch (high or low pressure) wired in series.
  5. To rule out the thermostat, pull it off and jumper R to Y and G at the wall or board. If the system runs, the thermostat or its settings are the problem.
Never jumper R to C. That shorts the transformer and blows the fuse, or the transformer itself.

The C wire

C is the other side of the transformer. It lets a smart or Wi‑Fi thermostat power itself. Without it, the thermostat "power steals" by leaking current through Y, W, or G, which causes resets, blank screens, Wi‑Fi dropouts, and relays or fans that chatter or cycle on their own.

  • First look for an unused conductor tucked back in the wall and at the air handler. Hook it to C on both ends.
  • No spare conductor: pull new 18/5 or 18/8, or use an add-a-wire or C-wire adapter kit.
  • Moving the G wire to C works in a pinch, but the customer loses independent fan control. Tell them before you do it.

Common faults

Thermostat screen blank

Check: dead batteries, then R to C at the board. Look for a blown fuse, tripped float switch, or open blower door switch.

Fuse keeps blowing

Check: a shorted wire. Staples through the cable, chewed wires, or a Y/C line at the condenser nicked by a trimmer are the usual causes. Also ohm the contactor coil. Disconnect wires one leg at a time to find which circuit is shorted.

Heat pump heats when it should cool

Check: the thermostat is set to O but the unit needs B, or the reverse. Most brands use O (valve energized in cool). Rheem and Ruud use B (valve energized in heat).

Blower won't shut off

Check: fan set to On at the thermostat, a G wire shorted to R, or a stuck relay on the board.

Electric heat runs but no airflow

Check: the thermostat is set for gas heat, so it isn't sending G with W. Change the heat type setting to electric.

Short cycling

Check: thermostat location (sun, supply register, drafty wall), cycle rate or differential settings, and power stealing on a thermostat without a C wire.

Replacing a thermostat

  • Take a photo of the old wiring before you disconnect anything, and label the wires.
  • Wire colors mean nothing on their own. Go by the terminal each wire lands on at the equipment.
  • Rh and Rc with a jumper: one transformer, leave the jumper. Two transformers (like a boiler plus a separate AC): remove the jumper and keep them separate.
  • Old mercury thermostats need proper disposal. Don't toss them in the truck trash.
  • If the old thermostat is 120V or 240V (baseboard heat), it's a line-voltage thermostat. None of this applies, so kill the power.
  • Set the equipment type, O/B, heat type, and stages in the new thermostat's installer menu before you leave.

Wire

Thermostat cable is 18 gauge. It's sold by conductor count: 18/2, 18/5, 18/8. Pull more conductors than you need. A spare C or a second stage later costs nothing now. Rooftop units use the same letters, usually with Y1/Y2 and W1/W2 for staging.
